How LitJev routes between fast and slow thinking

One prefill and one branch forward read the fast answer. A small trained decision head sits beside the same hidden state and judges whether slow thinking would pay off. The backbone is frozen; the slow path is the backbone's own thinking mode; every final answer still comes from the readout.

What the decision head learns

Not "is the fast answer right" but the joint outcome of both paths. Because the slow path is the frozen backbone's native thinking, its behaviour is fixed, so labels come for free: run both paths on each question and compare with the gold answer.

ClassFastSlowMeaning
0rightrightno need to think
1rightwrongdo not think; it would hurt
2wrongrightthink
3wrongwrongthinking will not help

Routing rule: P(2) − P(1) > λ. Changing the escalation rate means changing λ, not retraining. The head is a two-layer MLP over the selected layers' hidden states plus a few statistics of the candidate distribution (max probability, entropy, margin, question type). Its parameter count is negligible next to the backbone.

Why the slow prompt is built separately

First numbers

Qwen3.5-4B, 1500 MMLU-Pro questions across all 14 subjects, 1024 thinking tokens, evaluated on three held-out subjects (346 questions). Thinking helped on 24.6% of them and hurt on 4.6%.

PolicyEscalationAccuracy
Fast only0%0.540
Slow only100%0.740
Stats-only head, λ = 048%0.682
Layer 24 + PCA-64 head, λ = 053%0.717
Layer 24 + PCA-64 head, λ = −0.184%0.728
The hidden-state head beats the stats-only floor at every escalation rate, by about three points; routing AUROC 0.72 versus 0.70. With a twenty-point gap between fast and slow, a router at that AUROC keeps most of the gain at half the thinking cost rather than matching slow-only. Thinking still hit the budget on 90% of questions, so slow accuracy is a floor. One seed, one checkpoint: a pipeline check, not a benchmark.
